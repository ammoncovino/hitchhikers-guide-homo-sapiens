CHAPTER EIGHT

# The Policing Empire

How Ancient Patterns Run Modern States

The altar became a situation room. The tithe became a tax.

*The ancient patterns did not end, they changed their clothes. An empire that describes itself as a protector is examined, along with the currency that made the protection compulsory, the deaths the arrangement produced, and a dog whose death explains why a name has to be attached to every decision before it is made.*

> The ancient patterns did not end. They changed their clothes. The altar became a situation room. The priest became a press secretary. The covenant became a defense treaty. The tithe became a tax. The crusade became a preemptive strike. Same architecture. Contemporary materials.

### Hundreds of Rooms

Counts vary, and the variation is not evasion, it is definitional. A base may mean a persistent operational installation. A site may mean a smaller facility, an access location, or a separate property record inside one installation. The Congressional Research Service, reporting in July 2024, identifies sixty-eight persistent overseas bases in its principal accounting while discussing other sites and access arrangements separately; broader Defense Department property inventories produce far larger numbers. [12]

So the honest claim is not a number. It is that no other contemporary state operates anything comparable, by any of the definitions, and that the disagreement is only ever about how to count a thing nobody disputes the existence of.

Apply the Flip: what does a system that claims to protect actually do when the protected nation acts against the protector’s interests? Ask Iraq. Under the United Nations Oil-for-Food Programme it shifted the programme’s accounting from dollars to euros in November 2000. The invasion began in March 2003, under claims about weapons of mass destruction that were never found. [13]

The sequence is real. The causal link is disputed. Both things can be true, and the case against that invasion never needed the currency theory to stand up.

However you count them, each one is a room. And the room is always teaching.

### Rome, and the Word for What Rome Did

Rome did not describe itself as an occupier. It described itself as the provider of order. Pax Romana, the Roman peace, was not marketing invented afterward by historians. It was the operating claim, made at the time, in official language: we are not taking, we are stabilising, and what you are paying for is the stability.

The phrase is worth keeping in the original, because it survived. Pax Romana is Latin for the Roman peace, and later empires reached for the same construction deliberately. Pax Britannica for the century Britain ran the sea lanes. Pax Americana for the arrangement since 1945. In every case the word for what is being provided is peace, the word for who is providing it is the name of the provider, and the two are set side by side so that they cannot be separated by anyone living underneath them.

The claim was partly true, which is what made it work. The roads were real. The courts functioned. Trade moved safely across distances that had been lethal a century earlier. A province genuinely did receive something.

The problem was structural rather than moral, and it shows up in one question: could a province decline? Could a city that felt adequately peaceful already say thank you, we are fine, please take the garrison home? It could not, and the moment a protection cannot be refused it has stopped being protection and become something else that has kept the name.

> Every durable system organizes itself around a center it did not create and cannot finally own. Rome called that center order. The United States calls it freedom. Neither invented the thing they named, and neither can hold the deed to it, which is why both had to keep claiming to be its protector rather than its owner. The claim is always the same, and the failure is always the same: the day control gets mistaken for legitimacy.

Run the comparison and it is uncomfortably close. Both expanded the boundary of the thing they were protecting until it happened to match wherever they already had troops. Both maintained that the garrisons were requested. Both operated a currency the provinces were required to use and both derived enormous advantage from that requirement while describing it as convenience. Both told themselves, sincerely, that the alternative to their presence was chaos, and both were sometimes right, which is exactly what made the arrangement so difficult to argue with from inside it.

Set the two side by side properly, because the correlations are specific rather than atmospheric.

Rome extracted from the edges and narrated order at the center. Tribute flowed inward from the provinces; the story flowed outward from the capital, and the story was always about what the provinces were receiving. The United States has run the same arrangement with different instruments: the extraction is financial rather than agricultural, and the narration is media rather than monument, but the direction of travel is identical in both.

Rome maintained its legions where its interests were and called the arrangement protection. So does this one, at a scale Rome could not have imagined, and with the same insistence that the garrisons are welcome.

Rome ran bread and circuses. That phrase is usually quoted as contempt for the crowd, which gets it exactly backwards. Grain distribution and public spectacle were pressure-management technology. They reduced the felt urgency of a structural problem without touching the structure, and they worked, which is why they lasted. Any system that can lower the temperature without addressing the cause will do that indefinitely, because it is cheaper. Ask what performs that function now and the answers are not hard to find.

Rome used spectacle to make its own logic visible and acceptable at the same time. The arena was not a distraction from imperial values. It was imperial values, staged, with the outcome of disobedience shown in public to an audience that had paid to be there.

> And the failure mode is the same one in both cases: a system that has been continuous for a long time begins to treat continuity as legitimacy. We have always been here becomes we are entitled to be here, and the two are not the same claim. One is a fact about duration. The other is a claim about right, and nothing about lasting a long time establishes it.

The people writing under Roman occupation had a word for the pattern, and they did not use Rome’s name. They called it Babylon: luxury built on extraction, wealth separated from accountability, and a culture intoxicated enough not to look at what paid for it. That was not a prophecy about a city. It was a category. Rome was Babylon, and every system that reproduces Rome’s pattern becomes Babylon again, which is why the description keeps fitting.

And Rome did not fall the way most people imagine. The invasions came late, and they came to something already hollow. What came first is the subject of the next chapter: the guard created to protect the center worked out that it could sell it instead.

### 1971, When the Map Changed

On August 15, 1971, Nixon announced that the United States would no longer convert dollars to gold at a fixed rate. Through the 1970s, American and Saudi security and investment arrangements funnelled oil revenues into dollar-denominated assets, and oil stayed priced in dollars. Any nation that needed oil needed dollars. The dollar became not just a currency but the medium through which global energy flowed. The United States did not own the oil. It owned the channel. [14]

One correction worth making, because the popular version overstates it: there was never a single formal agreement requiring Saudi Arabia to price oil exclusively in dollars, and no such agreement expired. What held the channel open was not a contract. It was convention, market depth, security guarantees, and the absence of anywhere else to go, which is harder to leave than a contract would have been.

> The gold standard said: this paper represents something real we possess. The petrodollar said: this paper is the only thing you’re allowed to use to buy the thing you cannot survive without. One is backed by a commodity. The other is backed by a gun.

### The Death Numbers

Count the whole century and the number is enormous beyond argument. Estimates for twentieth-century war deaths run from around a hundred and ten million counting direct violence to well over two hundred million once war-induced famine and disease are included, and the two World Wars account for most of it. [15]

Narrow it to the period since 1945, the era the winners named the long peace, and the honest answer becomes tens of millions, and the imprecision is the point. There is no agreed total. Different datasets count different conflicts, different date ranges, and different kinds of death, and the figure moves by tens of millions depending on whether you include the famines, the epidemics, and the collapsed infrastructure that follow a war and kill for a decade after the shooting stops. [15]

Sit with that for a second. We know the serial number of the missile. We know which vessel carries which loadout, to the unit. We do not know, to the nearest twenty million, how many people have been killed by war in the lifetime of most people reading this.

> That is not a gap in the data. That is a statement of priorities, written in what got counted.

Apply the Flip anyway, because the conclusion survives the uncertainty: a system that produces mass death continuously across its entire period of supposed dominance, while narrating each new war as the exception required to preserve the peace, is not a peace system with a violence problem. It is a violence system with a peace narrative.

### Narrative Control

American soldiers killed in combat receive names, photographs, hometowns. The people killed by American military actions in other countries receive, if they receive anything, a number. The hundred and twenty-three children in Chapter Four. Do you know a single one of their names? The naming asymmetry is the control.

### Loki’s Law

There is a dog buried in this argument, and he belongs here.

Loki was my dog. A French bulldog, two years old, and he died of heat exposure at my own house. Not from cruelty. Nobody hurt him. He died from a handoff, because his care passed from one person to another and the single piece of information that would have saved him did not pass with it. Everyone in that chain was doing what they thought they were supposed to be doing. The gap was between them.

The policy written afterward carries his name, and its first rule is the only rule that matters here. One animal, one name. Not a team. Not a shift. A person. The animal’s name goes on that person’s badge, worn where guests can see it. Responsibility never transfers by assumption: a handoff requires the receiving person to say out loud that they have accepted it, in writing, and until they do the original keeper still owns the animal. Never unassigned. Not for an hour. [16]

The part that carries his name is called the lifeline, and it is a physical object rather than a principle. The keeper wears a badge, and on that badge is a small tag for every animal in their care that day: sloth, lemur, python, turtle. The animal has a matching badge at its own enclosure with the keeper’s name on it, so that any visitor standing in front of it can read who is responsible for the animal in front of them, by name, today. Sometimes there are two of these and sometimes three, because the line has to be visible from both ends.

And the keeper is not tied to that spot. They are expected to walk the whole park and to notice anything wrong anywhere in it, and to go and deal with it. What does not move is the responsibility. The animals on the badge stay on the badge wherever the person goes, so that the freedom to help everywhere never becomes an excuse for the one place that was actually theirs.

Now hold that next to Minab. A classification sat in a database saying that a building was a legitimate target. It had been true once. It stopped being true. It passed from system to system, review to review, and the one piece of information that would have saved a hundred and twenty-three children did not pass with it. Every individual in that chain was doing their job.

> A dog died because responsibility had a gap in it. A hundred and twenty-three children died because responsibility had a gap in it. The mechanism is identical and the only difference is scale, which is precisely why the fix has to be identical too. One target, one name. Never unassigned. Not for an hour.

Which brings up the thing that has to be said now rather than in ten years, while the arrangement is still being decided.

Artificial intelligence is already inside targeting. It sorts imagery, ranks candidates, maintains classifications, flags patterns no human has time to review. Some of that is genuinely good: a machine that never gets tired and never skips the checklist is exactly what a stale database classification needed and did not have.

But there is one position it cannot be allowed to occupy, and it is the position on the badge.

It is worth being exact about the words here, because the sloppy version of this argument is what lets the dangerous version through. A machine does not decide anything. A machine diagnoses. It sorts, ranks, matches, and returns the option that scores highest, and that is genuinely useful and often better than a tired human at three in the morning. But a diagnosis is not a decision. A decision is the act of accepting the consequence, and only a thing that can suffer a consequence is capable of making one.

A name on a decision is not paperwork. It is the whole mechanism. It works because a person can be asked, can be shamed, can be prosecuted, can be made to sit in a room with a parent, and can lie awake. Every one of those is a real consequence and every one of them requires a human being on the other end of it. An AI can be audited, corrected, retrained, and switched off, and none of those are accountability. They are maintenance.

So the danger is not that the machine will decide to kill someone. The danger is far more ordinary: that it becomes the place responsibility goes to disappear. The system did it. The model flagged it. The algorithm assessed the risk as acceptable. Every one of those sentences is a handoff with nobody on the receiving end, and we have already seen what a handoff with nobody on the receiving end does to a dog on a hot deck.

> A machine may name the recommendation. A human being must own the consequence. If those two ever come apart, we will have built the first system in history that can kill at scale with nobody’s name on it, and we will have done it by accident, out of convenience, one reasonable-looking delegation at a time.

Loki Law was written for animals in a park. It is ten rules long and a child could follow it. There is no technical reason the same standard cannot be applied to a weapon, and no honest reason it has not been.

> The machine is not evil. Evil requires intention and individual agency. The machine is a system doing what systems do: perpetuating itself, growing when it can, eliminating what threatens it, and telling the people inside it that they are free.

The next chapter examines what happens when the machine’s own protection apparatus gets captured from the inside. Because that, it turns out, is the oldest threat a working system faces.
