CHAPTER THREE

# Words Are Spells

How Language Becomes Control, and How to Become a Steward of What Passes Through You

Collateral damage.

Two words. Professional, bloodless, faintly technical. It passes through a listener cleanly and leaves almost nothing behind, which is why you can hear it in a briefing, read it in a report, and go on with your afternoon.

It means children.

It means the family in the house beside the target, and the schoolteacher walking home, and a backpack in a pile of concrete. The phrase carries none of that, and the reason it carries none of that is that it was built not to. Somebody sat down and constructed a term for the killing of civilians that would not make a listener picture a civilian, and it worked so well that it is now used by people who have never once thought about where it came from.

> That is compression, and compression is not shortening. It is the removal of the part that would have made you feel something, performed so smoothly that you never notice the removal.

And it does not happen in one move. That is the thing to understand about it, because a single dishonest word can be caught. Compression works by increments. The full description gets shortened to a phrase. The phrase gets shortened to a term. The term becomes routine, and routine words are not examined, and once a word is no longer examined it can be moved slightly without anyone objecting. Then it is moved again.

Civilian deaths becomes collateral damage becomes acceptable loss becomes a number in a column. Nobody lied at any step. Each step was only slightly shorter than the one before it, and by the end the sentence can be spoken in a room full of decent people without a single one of them seeing a child.

Watch the same mechanism run on the largest word we have. War used to be a thing you declared, formally, in front of your own population, who then had to be persuaded to send their sons. Then it became a police action. Then an intervention. Then an operation, with a name chosen by a committee. Then kinetic activity. Then, in the current vocabulary, simply strikes, which sounds like weather.

The killing did not change. The word for it was shortened, step by step, until the population it belonged to could no longer feel what it referred to, and each shortening was reasonable on its own terms and defensible if you challenged it directly. That is the entire method. Little by little, until the agenda arrives, and it arrives in a room where nobody thinks anything unusual has been said.

> The word is not the thing. But the word decides whether you can think about the thing at all, and a word can be adjusted a great deal faster than a person can notice.

### Gospel, and What a Spell Actually Is

There is a word sitting in plain sight in English that shows the whole mechanism working, and almost nobody has looked at it closely, because it is a word people mostly encounter in church.

> Gospel.

It is Old English. Gōd-spell. And the two halves are exactly what they look like: gōd, good, and spell, which in Old English did not mean an incantation and did not mean arranging letters. It meant a story. An account. A telling. So the word is, with no cleverness required, the good story. [5]

Now follow spell forward, because it goes somewhere.

A spell in Old English was a spoken thing, and specifically a spoken thing with weight: not chatter, but a telling that did something to the person receiving it. That is why the same word gave us the other spell, the one a witch casts. Not a coincidence and not folk etymology. It is one word, and the reason it could become an incantation is that it already meant an arrangement of words intended to have an effect.

One correction, because the popular version of this overreaches and this book has been careful elsewhere. Spelling, as in arranging letters, is a different word. That one arrives later, from Old French, and it has nothing to do with the first. So the tidy line you sometimes hear, that spelling and casting a spell are the same act, is not true.

What is true is more useful. Gospel and incantation are the same word.

> A thousand years ago, English had one word covering both a story told to change you and a form of words spoken to produce an effect, because it had not occurred to anyone that these were different activities. Then Christianity took that word for its central text, and the older sense drifted off into the part of the language reserved for magic, and now they sit at opposite ends of the dictionary looking unrelated.

Then look at what the Greek underneath it was doing, because this is where it stops being etymology and becomes politics.

Gospel translates euangelion, good news. And euangelion was not a religious word when the New Testament writers picked it up. It was an imperial one. It was the term for an official announcement from the emperor: a victory in the field, the birth of an heir, the accession of a new Caesar. Inscriptions from the period use it for Augustus. It arrived by herald, it was read aloud in the forum, and it told a province what had happened and what it now meant.

So when a handful of provincials under military occupation began circulating an euangelion about an executed laborer, they were not choosing devotional language. They were taking the word the empire used for its own proclamations and putting a crucified man in it, which everyone hearing it in that century would have understood immediately as an act of substitution. [10]

> Every word in that chain is doing the same job. A story told to change a person, an arrangement of words with an effect, an official announcement of who is now in charge. Gospel, spell, and euangelion are three names for one activity, and this book is about that activity from beginning to end.

Every word you accept into your vocabulary is an agreement. When you accept a word, you accept the frame that came attached to it, usually without inspecting either.

### The Shrinking Dictionary

In Orwell’s 1984, the government didn’t primarily control people through violence. It controlled them through language. Newspeak, a language being deliberately reduced year by year. If you don’t have the word for a thought, you cannot think the thought. Language is the scaffolding thought climbs. Remove the scaffolding, the thought cannot be built.

Watch what happens when a culture replaces precision with slogans. Watch what happens when jargon replaces plain description. Enhanced interrogation. Quantitative easing. Ethnic cleansing. Downsizing. Each phrase takes something that would produce a visceral response in plain language and wraps it in abstraction until it can be discussed in a meeting room without anyone having to feel what they’re talking about.

The same mechanism runs through spiritual language. Higher self. Vibration. Divine timing. It’s all energy. These phrases pack enormous, unexamined claims into a few syllables. Everyone nodding along assumes the others mean the same thing, when almost no one does. A biological claim, my body is running low on what it needs, is a claim you can act on and a claim that can be wrong. My higher self is testing me is a claim you can only sit with, and can never be shown to be false.

### The Three Monkeys, What They Actually Mean

Almost everyone knows the image: three monkeys, one covering its eyes, one covering its ears, one covering its mouth. Almost everyone has it backwards. The modern interpretation is a permission structure: look away, don’t cause conflict. The original meaning was a discipline, a three-stage process for handling information with integrity.

And the story of how it got turned around is the best proof this chapter has, because the drift is still visible in the words.

The carving everyone knows sits above a stable door at the Tōshō-gū shrine in Nikkō, Japan, from the first half of the seventeenth century, attributed to the sculptor Hidari Jingoro. It is one panel of eight depicting the stages of a human life, and the philosophy behind it arrived from China centuries earlier, carrying a line of Confucius: look not at what is contrary to propriety, listen not, speak not. [4]

In Japanese the maxim is mizaru, kikazaru, iwazaru, see not, hear not, speak not. The -zaru on the end of each is an old negative ending. It also happens to sound exactly like -zaru, the combining form of saru, the Japanese word for monkey. [4]

> The teaching had nothing to do with monkeys. The monkeys are a pun. A grammatical accident in one language turned an instruction about how to handle information into three animals covering their faces.

Then look at what English did on top of that. The Japanese phrase contains no word for evil. See not, hear not, speak not, that is the whole of it. Evil was added in translation, and the moment it was added the instruction inverted. A discipline about how to receive and transmit accurately became a rule about what to avoid looking at.

Two drifts, in two languages, on the same short phrase. One turned a teaching into an image. The other turned a practice into a prohibition. Nobody had to lie for either one to happen. That is the point of this entire chapter: language does not need a conspiracy to end up meaning the opposite of what it started as. It only needs time and a little convenience.

Which is why it is worth putting back the way it was.

> Information should become more accurate as it moves through a human. That is the whole standard. Most information does the opposite, and the three stages are three places where it goes wrong.

**STAGE ONE. SEE NO EVIL: OBSERVE BEFORE YOU INTERPRET.**

The instruction is not to look away. It is to separate what you actually saw from what you expected to see, which is harder than it sounds, because the mind does not deliver those two things separately. It delivers one merged package and labels the whole thing observation.

You have experienced this. Two people watch the same argument and leave with two different accounts, and neither of them is lying. They saw the same event and stored different things, because each was running a different expectation while watching. The tone one of them heard as defensive, the other heard as tired.

So the first discipline is a question asked before any conclusion: what did I actually see, as distinct from what I assumed was happening? Almost nobody does this, because the interpretation arrives so fast that it feels like part of the perception.

Failure signature: I saw it, therefore it is true.

**STAGE TWO. HEAR NO EVIL: UNDERSTAND BEFORE YOU INTERNALIZE.**

You can hear a sentence, repeat it back word for word, and have no idea what it means. Repetition is not comprehension. Most of what people believe was acquired at exactly this stage: heard often enough, in a trusted enough voice, that it slid past the checkpoint and got filed as known.

The discipline here is to ask not only what a claim means but how it works. What is the mechanism? What would have to be true for this to be false? A claim that cannot fail is not a strong claim, it is an unfalsifiable one, and that difference matters more than almost anything else in this book.

This is also the stage where identity gets installed ahead of understanding. Someone adopts a label, a side, a framework, and from that point forward the framework does the thinking for them. It is efficient. It is also the precise moment a person stops being able to update.

Failure signature: it sounds right, therefore it must be right.

**STAGE THREE. SPEAK NO EVIL: VERIFY BEFORE YOU TRANSMIT.**

Everything you pass along, you amplify. That is not a metaphor. When you repeat a claim you lend it your credibility, and the person who hears it from you does not receive it as a stranger’s assertion. They receive it as something you vouched for.

So the third discipline is to transmit only what survived the first two, and to mark the difference between what you know, what you believe, and what you heard. Those are three different things, and collapsing them is how rumor acquires the texture of fact.

Notice what social media does to this stage. It is an architecture built to reward the opposite. The content that travels furthest is the content that produces the strongest feeling fastest, and feeling is not one of the three tests. A platform that measures sharing rather than accuracy is a machine for inverting the third monkey, at a scale no shrine carving ever anticipated.

Failure signature: it feels true, therefore I should share it.

Run all three and you become something specific, and it is worth naming, because the word for it is not believer and it is not skeptic.

> A steward. Someone who takes responsibility for what passes through them. Whose job is to reduce drift, preserve signal, expose mechanism, and hand on what arrived without the fingerprints of their own expectations pressed into it.

And the inversion is not hypothetical. In the healthy version, truth enters and truth exits. In the inverted version, truth enters, becomes narrative at the first stage, becomes belief at the second, becomes rhetoric at the third, and gets transmitted as though it were the original observation. Somewhere downstream a person receives that rhetoric and calls it something they saw with their own eyes.

Nobody lied at any point in that chain. Everyone simply repeated something before they understood it.

### The Alpha-Omega Inversion

You are always shown the Omega, the institution, the doctrine, the flag, the ritual. It feels like nature. You are never shown the Alpha. Where it started, who built it, what problem it was actually solving. A recurring control pattern follows this progression: Need produces a Story. The story attracts a Cult. The cult survives long enough to become a Religion. The religion embeds into Culture. The culture persists long enough to feel like Nature. By the time it reaches Nature, no one inside the system can see the Alpha anymore.

> The Omega is what they show you. The Alpha is what they built it to hide.

See what is actually there, not what you expect. Understand the mechanism, not just the conclusion. Speak only what has been verified. And when you encounter any system powerful enough to feel like nature, trace it back. Find the beginning. Find the need it was built to serve. Everything else is commentary.
